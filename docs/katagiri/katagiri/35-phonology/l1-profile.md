# Your interference profile

Built from Ukrainian (native), English (C2), Finnish (studied). Confidence noted per item, because some of this is well-established contrastive phonology and some is my prediction.

**Headline:** you arrive with an unusually good hand for Japanese. Ukrainian gives you the consonants that break English speakers; Finnish gives you the timing system that breaks everyone. Your main risks come from Ukrainian *prosody* and — counterintuitively — from your English being too good.

---

## Free wins — do not spend time on these

Generic courses will drill these for weeks. Skip them, verify once, move on.

### ら行 — the flap *(high confidence)*
Ukrainian р is an alveolar tap/trill. Japanese ら is an alveolar flap. You already own this articulation. English speakers spend months here.

**Only correction:** one tap, not a trill. Ukrainian р often rolls; Japanese ら never does. Lighter and single.

### つ — the ts affricate *(high confidence)*
Ukrainian ц is exactly this, and unlike English you have it word-initially — цукор, ціна. つ、つき、つくえ should be immediate. This is one of the top three English-speaker failures and it's free for you.

### Palatalization — きゃ・にゃ・ひゃ・し・ち *(high confidence)*
Ukrainian has a full palatalized series (ть дь нь сь ль). Japanese palatal onsets and the ゃゅょ series will feel natural rather than exotic. Finnish doesn't help here but doesn't hurt.

### No articles, no obligatory subject *(high confidence)*
Ukrainian has neither. Japanese has neither. English speakers agonize over "is it *the* apple or *an* apple" and over dropping subjects; you won't. Japanese omits everything inferable, which is normal to you.

### Cases → particles *(high confidence)*
This is the big grammatical one. English speakers must first learn what a case *is*. You have seven, and Finnish gave you fifteen.

| Japanese | Ukrainian | Finnish |
|---|---|---|
| を | знахідний (acc.) | partitive / accusative |
| が | називний (nom.) | nominative |
| に (location, recipient) | давальний / місцевий | allative / adessive |
| で (instrument) | орудний | instructive-ish / adessive |
| から | — (з + gen.) | ablative -lta |
| の | родовий (gen.) | genitive -n |

Japanese particles are postpositions doing case work. You already think this way.

### 私には〜があります *(high confidence)*
Finnish `minulla on kirja` is structurally the same move as 私に本があります — possession expressed as location plus existence, not as a "have" verb. English speakers find Japanese possession weird. You have a template.

### Kana literacy *(high confidence)*
You're already biliterate (Cyrillic + Latin) and Ukrainian orthography is strongly phonemic. Kana is perfectly phonemic. Your prior is "the script tells you the sound and means it," which is exactly right for kana and exactly wrong for English. Expect kana to take you well under ten hours.

## Your Finnish is the single best preparation for the hardest thing in Japanese

Mora timing is what makes learners hard to understand and it's the thing Slavic and Germanic speakers both fail. You've already learned it — in Finnish.

Finnish has **phonemic vowel length** and **phonemic consonant gemination**, and uses both to distinguish real words. So does Japanese. The mapping is direct:

| Finnish | Japanese | Same principle |
|---|---|---|
| tuli / tuuli | ここ / こうこう | vowel length is lexical |
| tuli / tulli | きて / きって | consonant length is lexical |
| kuka / kukka | かこ / かっこ | gemination is a full beat |
| — | おばさん / おばあさん | 4 moras vs 5 |

**Teaching consequence:** I'm going to teach you mora timing *by analogy to Finnish quantity*, not from scratch. When you meet っ, the instruction is "this is Finnish gemination." When you meet おう, it's "this is Finnish `uu`." That's not a mnemonic — it's the same phonological category, and you have working motor programs for it.

Finnish also has no vowel reduction and weak stress, both of which match Japanese and neither of which English does.

This is worth stating plainly: you got lucky. Most people arrive at Japanese with no concept that length can be lexical, and it costs them years of being politely misunderstood.

## Real targets — in priority order

### 1. Pitch accent vs your stress reflex *(high confidence — this is your #1 risk)*

Ukrainian has free lexical stress, so you already accept that a suprasegmental feature can distinguish words. Good.

The problem is *how* you realize it. Ukrainian stress is **dynamic** — louder, longer, more clearly articulated. Japanese accent is **pitch only** — same volume, same duration, the voice just drops.

So the predicted error is specific and nasty: you'll mark 箸 [1] by making は **louder and longer**, which simultaneously produces a wrong accent *and* breaks the mora timing you're otherwise good at. One habit damaging two systems.

**Drill:** produce はし [1] and はし [2] at identical volume and identical duration, changing only pitch. Hum the contour first with no consonants, then add them back. Verify with the pitch-contour tool when it exists. Watch specifically for length creep on the accented mora.

### 2. う — unrounded *(high confidence)*

Ukrainian у and Finnish u are both fully rounded, lips protruded. Japanese う is compressed/unrounded — lips spread or neutral, tongue high and central. Rounded う is a persistent, immediately audible foreign marker, and it's in です, ます, つ, and every ~う verb, so it's everywhere.

**Drill:** say ウ with a slight smile. If your lips protrude, you're saying у.

### 3. ふ — bilabial *(high confidence)*

You have ф [f] (labiodental) and х [x] (velar) but not [ɸ] (bilabial). ふ is lips against lips, like blowing out a candle. Both your available substitutes are wrong, and Ukrainian х is a *different* wrong from the English one.

### 4. し・ち — not retracted *(medium confidence)*

Ukrainian ш and ч are postalveolar and non-palatalized (unlike Russian's soft ч). Japanese し is [ɕi], further forward and palatalized. Predicted error: し comes out too "hard," closer to ши. Your own сь is closer to the target than your ш is — build し from сь, not from ш.

### 5. Devoiced vowels *(high confidence — universal, no L1 helps)*

です → "des", ます → "mas", 好き → "ski", しました → "shimashta". Nothing in Ukrainian, Finnish, or English prepares you. Straight memorization.

### 6. お and い precision *(low confidence, minor)*

Ukrainian о is more open [ɔ]; Japanese お is closer to [o]. Ukrainian и is lax [ɪ]; Japanese い is tense [i] — use і, not и. Small, cosmetic, fix late.

## The counterintuitive one: your English is a liability here

C2 English is your strongest non-native language, which means it's probably your default "speaking a foreign language" motor mode. Three concrete costs:

### Katakana loanwords *(high confidence — this is the sneaky one)*

You know what the English word is, so you'll reach for the English pronunciation. But コンピューター is `ko-n-pyu-u-ta-a` (6 moras), マクドナルド is `ma-ku-do-na-ru-do` (6), ビル is not "building."

A Japanese person who has never heard English will not understand your English "computer." **On loanwords specifically, your English makes you less comprehensible than someone who never learned it.** These are frequent, and the error is invisible to you because you feel like you already know the word.

**Rule going in the vault:** loanwords are treated as *new Japanese words*, with mora counts recorded and no reference to the English source. `register: loanword` tag, and the mora count is drilled explicitly.

### English prosody leaking *(medium confidence)*

English is stress-timed with heavy schwa reduction. Japanese has neither. Ukrainian and Finnish are both closer to Japanese here than English is — so the instruction is: **when you speak Japanese, come at it from Ukrainian, not from English.** If you feel yourself in "foreign language mode," that mode is English-shaped and it's the wrong shape.

### English-language materials *(high confidence)*

Nearly all Japanese learning material is written for English speakers, so it drills your free wins (ら, つ, palatalization) and under-drills your real problems (う rounding, dynamic stress). Hepburn romaji encodes English vowel assumptions that are worse for you than for an English speaker.

Which is one more argument for the setting you already picked: skip romaji, go straight to kana.

## は/が may be genuinely easier for you *(medium confidence — my prediction)*

は/が is the canonical multi-year struggle for English speakers, because English marks given/new information with articles and stress, and English word order is rigid.

You have two better tools:

- **Ukrainian free word order encodes theme–rheme.** Slavic linguistics teaches this explicitly — тема/рема. は marks the theme. You may already have a name for the thing English speakers can't perceive.
- **Finnish uses word order for definiteness and givenness** — `Kirja on pöydällä` vs `Pöydällä on kirja`. That contrast lives in almost exactly the territory は/が occupies.

So I'm revising the plan in [[g-wa-topic]]: instead of holding you at understanding level 1 for months on the assumption that it can't be explained, I'll try the theme–rheme framing early and see if it lands. If it does, you skip the most famous plateau in Japanese.

**Watch out for one aspect trap:** Ukrainian's perfective/imperfective distinction is grammaticalized and obligatory, and Japanese た is *tense*, not perfective. You'll be tempted to map доконаний вид onto た and ている onto недоконаний. It half-works and then betrays you — Japanese ている covers both progressive and resultant-state, which Ukrainian splits differently. Flag any confusion here in the error museum; I expect this to be a recurring pattern for you specifically.
